A dense detector predicts one vector per grid cell. Training needs the same layout as a target: the cell that contains the centre of the box is responsible for the object, every other cell must say "no object". Drag the shapes (corner handle = resize) and watch grid_targets fill the tensor.

what grid_targets leaves in each object's cell (the final tensor, after all objects were written in order)

Try this - predict first, then check

  1. Drag the small square into the circle's cell (or switch to G = 4). What will T hold for that cell: both boxes, their average, or one of them? Read the table.
    why

    A cell has room for one box. grid_targets writes the objects in order, so the later box overwrites the earlier one, while the class part is only ever set to 1, never cleared: the cell ends up "two-hot" with the second object's box. dense_loss takes the argmax of the class part (the first 1), so the cell may be trained on one object's box with the other object's class, and the first object is invisible to training. YOLOv2 answered with several anchors per cell, YOLOv3 / FPN with several grids of different strides.

  2. Now set G = 16. Is the collision gone? What happened to positive cells / all cells?
    why

    Cells are 8 px, so the centres land in different cells. But 3 objects now make 3 positive cells out of 256 (about 1%): 99% of the objectness channel says "no object". Real dense detectors have tens of thousands of cells or anchors per image and a handful of objects; section 5 (focal loss) deals with exactly this imbalance.

  3. Drag the circle slowly to the right across a grid line while watching channel 1 (cx) and the highlighted cell.
    why

    cx changes smoothly (it is normalised to the image), but responsibility jumps to the neighbouring cell within one pixel. Two cells that see almost the same pixels get opposite objectness targets, which is why YOLOv1's boxes near cell borders are unstable, and why later heads make several nearby locations positive (FCOS, section 6).

  4. At G = 16, make the triangle large with its corner handle. How many cells does it cover, and how many of them are positive?
    why

    One: the cell that holds the box centre. All the other cells that see most of the triangle must predict objectness 0, which is a hard target to learn. Note also that the "+" (box centre) is not the triangle's visual centre: the target is defined by the box, not by the shape.

  5. Predict T[..., 1] (cx) for the same object at G = 4 and at G = 16, then check.
    answer

    Identical: in this notebook the target box is normalised to the image, not to the cell. The cell index carries the coarse position; the model's decoding in section 2, cx = (j + sigmoid(t_x)) / G, maps its cell-relative output back into this layout.
