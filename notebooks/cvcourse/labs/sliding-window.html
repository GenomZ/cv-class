Sliding-window inference

D3-02 · section 6 All labs

A 200 x 200 image is predicted tile by tile, exactly like sliding_window_predict. The toy model is good in the middle of a tile and poor near its border (little context there). Overlap and blending decide whether those weak borders become seams; the stride decides the cost.

Try this - predict first, then check

  1. Set the stride equal to the tile size (no overlap). Where is the stitched quality worst, and why exactly there?
    why

    Along every tile border: a pixel at the edge of a tile was predicted with half of its neighbourhood missing. Without overlap each pixel is predicted once, so those weak predictions are the final answer and form a grid of seams. (The last row and column still overlap: the final tile is shifted back to end at the image border, as in sliding_window_predict.)

  2. Back to stride 48 (overlap 16) with uniform averaging. Are the seams gone? Try a smaller stride. Now switch to Gaussian.
    why

    Uniform averaging only dilutes the weak edge predictions: they still count as much as the good ones, so the worst inner pixel goes from 0.33 to 0.50 and stays there however small you make the stride. A Gaussian (or Hann) window gives each tile little weight near its own border, so the overlap is filled by the tile that has the pixel near its centre: 0.75 at 16 px overlap, 0.95 at 24 px. That is what nnU-Net and MONAI do (Gaussian, sigma = tile / 8).

  3. Predict the number of forward passes at stride 32, then 16, then 8 (tile 64). Check the cost chart.
    why

    The number of positions per axis grows like H / s, the passes like (H / s)2: halving the stride roughly quadruples the cost (16 → 36 → 100 → 324 here). Overlap of 25-50% of the tile is the usual compromise.

  4. Press play and watch the "tiles per pixel" map fill in. Which pixels end up seen by 4 tiles?
    why

    Pixels inside the overlap of two rows and two columns of tiles. The averaging divides by exactly this count (uniform) or by the summed window weights (Gaussian), so every pixel is a proper weighted mean.

  5. With Gaussian blending, set the context ramp to 24 (the model needs more context to be confident). How much overlap do you now need to hide the seams?
    why

    Roughly twice the ramp (ramp 12: 24 px overlap gives 0.95; ramp 24: 48 px gives 0.90). Each pixel must be at least one ramp length away from the border of some tile that covers it, and that tile must dominate the blend. Bigger receptive fields need bigger overlaps.
